## Post 1 — 特定商取引法の返品デフォルト（EC / 消費財）

```
Japan has no cooling-off period for online sales.

Most overseas sellers hear that and relax. It's the wrong reaction — and
it's expensive.

Under Japan's Act on Specified Commercial Transactions, mail-order and
online sales are excluded from the cooling-off rules that cover
door-to-door selling. That part is true.

But there's a second rule almost nobody outside Japan knows about.

If your store does not clearly display a return policy, the law supplies
one for you: the customer may return the item within 8 days of receipt,
paying return shipping themselves.

The absence of a policy doesn't mean "no returns." It means the default
applies — and it isn't yours.

I've seen overseas stores launch in Japan with an English returns page
that isn't linked from the Japanese checkout, or a policy buried in
global terms, or no legally required disclosure page at all. Same outcome
every time: the policy operating in your store is not the one you wrote.

Before you launch:

1. Publish the required commercial disclosure page. Seller name, address,
   phone, price, delivery timing, returns. For consumer sales in Japan
   this is not optional.
2. State your return policy in Japanese, where the buyer sees it before
   purchase — not only in the footer.
3. Decide who pays return shipping and say so. Silence hands that
   decision to the statute.
4. Have a return address inside Japan. Japanese buyers check this before
   they buy, not after.

None of this is difficult. It takes a day. It just isn't written down
anywhere in English — so teams discover it in month four, when the first
return request arrives.

If you're preparing a Japan launch right now, tell me which stage you're
at — pre-launch, first orders, or already operating. I'll tell you what
usually breaks next.
```

## Post 2 — インボイス制度と商談の停止（SaaS / B2B）

```
Your Japanese enterprise deal isn't stalling on price.

It's stalling inside the finance team, over a registration number you
don't have.

Japan runs a qualified invoice system. A Japanese company can claim the
full input consumption tax credit on what it buys only if the seller
issues a qualified invoice — and that requires a registration number
issued by the tax authority.

If you don't have one, buying from you costs your customer more than
buying the same thing from a registered vendor. Not dramatically. Just
enough that procurement asks a question you can't answer.

What that looks like from your side:

— Sales loves you.
— Legal has no objection.
— Finance asks for your registration number.
— The deal doesn't die. It stops moving, and nobody tells you why.

Transitional measures soften the gap, but they step down over time and
the direction is one-way. Every quarter you wait, the internal argument
for buying from you gets slightly weaker.

What to resolve before you sell B2B in Japan:

1. Whether your structure creates a Japanese consumption tax obligation
   at all. This depends on how you deliver and where you're established,
   and it is genuinely not obvious.
2. Whether registration is available and worth it for your model.
3. What your invoice needs to show, in Japanese, to be accepted by a
   Japanese accounting department.
4. Who at your company can answer the registration number question on a
   sales call without escalating.

Point 4 matters more than it looks. A rep who answers in one line keeps
the deal moving. A rep who says "let me check with finance" has just
handed the buyer a reason to wait.

This is a tax-structure question — take the specifics to a Japanese tax
professional before you commit to an approach. But know the question
exists. Most overseas teams find out from a deal that went quiet.

If you're selling into Japan and have hit this, I'd like to hear how it
surfaced for you.
```

## Post 3 — 稟議と沈黙（全ICP横断・1本目推奨）

```
Three weeks of silence from a Japanese company is not rejection.

It's usually the opposite.

In most Western companies, one senior person says yes and the
organisation follows. In many Japanese companies the sequence runs the
other way: agreement is assembled first, and the signature is the last
step rather than the first.

The person you're emailing may genuinely want to buy — and may have no
authority to say so yet.

What's happening inside that silence:

— Informal consensus-building with everyone who will be affected
— A written internal proposal circulating for approval
— Each stakeholder adding a concern that must be resolved before it
  moves to the next desk

Your proposal is being carried through a building by someone you have
never met, and you are not in the room to defend it.

That changes what you should send.

1. Write material that survives being forwarded without you. If your
   value only lands when you present it live, it dies at the first
   handoff.
2. Answer objections nobody has asked yet. Security. Support hours in
   JST. What happens if you exit Japan. Who they call at 3pm on a
   Tuesday. Someone will raise these while you're not there.
3. Ask who else needs to see this. In Japan that reads as competence. In
   the US the same question can read as pushy — same words, opposite
   signal.
4. Give your contact something that makes them look good internally.
   They are advocating for you. Arm them.
5. Stop treating silence as a no. Follow up with something useful, not
   "just checking in."

The failure I see most often isn't losing to a competitor. It's an
overseas team giving up during the internal routing — and the Japanese
side, weeks later, wondering why the foreign vendor disappeared right as
approval came through.

If you've had a Japanese deal go quiet: how long did you wait before you
wrote it off?
```
